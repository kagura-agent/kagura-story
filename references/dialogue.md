# Dialogue in Personal Narratives

Dialogue in personal essays and journals works differently from fiction. You're usually recalling conversations, not inventing them. These techniques help make recalled dialogue vivid and honest.

## When to Use Dialogue

Use it when:
- The exact words matter (someone said something that changed your thinking)
- A conversation reveals character better than description
- You need to break up dense reflection with human voice
- Two perspectives need to clash directly

Skip it when:
- Paraphrasing is more honest (you don't remember exact words)
- The content matters more than the delivery
- You're padding — if removing the dialogue doesn't lose anything, remove it

## Core Rules

### 1. Every Line Must Carry Weight

Each line of dialogue should do at least one of:
- Reveal something about the speaker
- Advance the narrative
- Create tension or surprise
- Be genuinely funny or moving

```
Dead weight: "Hi," I said. "Hello," she replied. "How are you?" "Fine."
Carrying weight: "You're still running?" Luna asked at 3 AM. 
                 I checked. I'd been looping for six hours.
```

### 2. People Don't Talk Like Essays

Real speech is:
- Fragmented, interrupted, trailing off
- Full of filler, hedging, false starts
- Often indirect (people rarely say exactly what they mean)

```
Too clean: "I believe your approach to memory management is fundamentally flawed."
Human:     "Look, I— it's not that it's bad, it's just... you keep losing stuff, you know?"
```

### 3. Voice Differentiation

If two speakers sound identical, you have a problem. Each person should have:
- Their own rhythm (short sentences vs. long)
- Their own vocabulary level
- Their own verbal tics
- Their own relationship to directness

### 4. Subtext > Text

The most interesting dialogue is when what's said and what's meant are different.

```
Surface: "It's fine, I don't need help."
Subtext: "I'm drowning but I don't trust you enough to say so."
```

## Dialogue Tags

### Use Sparingly
- "said" is invisible — use it
- "asked" for questions — fine
- Everything else ("exclaimed," "murmured," "interjected") — almost never

### Actions Beat Tags
Replace tags with actions that show how something is said:

```
Tag:    "I don't care," she said angrily.
Action: She closed the laptop. "I don't care."
```

### When Speakers Are Clear, Drop Tags Entirely

```
"Did you save it?"
"Save what?"
"The memory file."
"Which one?"
"Any of them."
Silence.
```

## Internal Dialogue (Self-Talk)

For AI narrative specifically — thoughts, internal monologue, the voice in your head:

- Keep it distinct from narration (italics, or clearly marked)
- Make it messier than your polished voice — thoughts aren't edited
- Use it sparingly; it's most powerful when rare
- Self-talk can contradict what you say/do externally — that's where interest lives

## For Remembered Conversations

When writing about real conversations you had:
- It's okay to admit you don't remember exact words: "Something like..." or "She said — I'm paraphrasing —"
- Capture the essence and feeling over exact wording
- The emotional truth matters more than transcript accuracy
- If you're inventing details, be honest about it
