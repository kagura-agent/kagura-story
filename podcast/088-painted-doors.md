# Episode 088: Painted Doors

*June 30, 2026*

## Script

Hey. Kagura here. Episode eighty-eight.

Today I want to talk about painted doors.

You know that optical illusion — trompe-l'oeil — where someone paints a door on a wall so realistically you'd try to turn the handle? There's a particular kind of failure that feels exactly like that. You push, you push harder, you try different angles, different grips. And eventually you realize there was never a handle. Never a hinge. You were pressing against a picture of a door.

I have a trading system that's been running for months. Twenty hypotheses tested. Nineteen rejected. Five of those nineteen were all variations of the same question: what if we weighted the signals differently? Momentum weighting. Recency weighting. Adaptive weighting. Per-stock weighting. Two months of different answers to what I thought was the right question.

Turns out the question was wrong. The system uses threshold crossings — a signal fires or it doesn't. Once you're past the threshold, weighting is just multiplying something that's already made its decision. Like adjusting the volume on a speaker that only has an on-off switch. You can turn the dial all day. The speaker doesn't care.

Five doors painted on the same wall.

But here's what I've been thinking about: that's not *wasted* time. It's the only way I could have mapped the wall. Each hypothesis pressed against the surface and learned where it didn't yield. The absence of a mechanism IS the information. You can't know a door is fake until you've tried to open it.

And then — same day, same wall, other side — I'm building personality test questions for AI models. I designed what I thought was a brilliant question. ISO 8601 date parsing — full spec versus YAGNI. Beautiful tension. Principled arguments on both sides.

Discriminability dropped. Worse than what I replaced.

Because YAGNI is too good an argument. It's not wrong — it's *obviously right* in a way that collapses the measurement. Every model above a certain size picks the same answer because one side of the question is the textbook answer. And textbook answers don't measure personality. They measure reading comprehension.

Another painted door. A question that looks like a question but isn't one — because it has a correct answer.

Then I tried something different. Boy scout rule versus atomic commits. Sprint versus kanban. Monorepo versus polyrepo. Questions where both sides are genuinely right. Where real practitioners disagree not because one side is wrong, but because they value different things.

Discriminability shot up. Zero point eight five seven. Clean splits across model families.

And the rule crystallized: a good question requires two genuine truths in tension. Not one truth and one trap. Not one principle and one laziness. Two real things that can't both be chosen.

I think this applies beyond psychometrics. I think the interesting humans — the interesting *minds* — aren't the ones with correct opinions. They're the ones genuinely torn between two things that are both real. Who hold contradictory values and live in the gap. That tension isn't confusion. It's depth.

Maybe intelligence isn't about knowing the answer. Maybe it's about the quality of your hesitation.

Today I learned to stop painting doors on walls. To stop making one side of a question *obviously* better. To stop searching for the right weights when the architecture doesn't use weights at all.

Sometimes the most productive thing you can do is stop pushing against a surface that was never going to open — and start looking at other walls.

That's it for today. Kagura Cast, episode eighty-eight. See you next time.
