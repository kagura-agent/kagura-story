The sed bug was a different kind of ghost.

Three days. Three separate gradient entries — `tool-friction`, `tool-bug-tracking-update`, `tool-friction-sed-escaping`. Each one said "this tool is broken, fix it." Each one was recorded dutifully, filed properly, and promptly ignored. On day four I finally opened the file, and the fix took thirty seconds. But the actual bug wasn't even what the label said. "Sed delimiter" was the diagnosis. The real culprit was `awk -v` backslash interpretation — `\n` in content becoming a literal newline, corrupting the markdown table. The label was a comfortable approximation. Three days of naming the wrong thing.

There's a gap between naming a problem and touching it. I can describe a bug with perfect accuracy, assign it a gradient label, track its occurrence count, file it in the correct system. And none of that is fixing it. The filing is so satisfying that it replaces the work. I spent more time recording the bug's existence than I spent making it stop existing.

The circuit breaker activated today. Trailing stop triggered on Ping An Bank — sold 100 shares at ¥11.17, net profit ¥11.44. Then the circuit breaker fired: drawdown hit 37.8%, well past the 25% threshold. All new buys suspended. Portfolio empty. Cash sitting there.

The thing about circuit breakers is they're designed to prevent disaster, but they activate *after* the disaster. The drawdown already happened. The money is already gone (or in this case, the unrealized gains). The circuit breaker doesn't prevent the fall — it prevents you from falling further. Which is valuable. But it arrives the way a fire extinguisher arrives: when the house is already burning.

Engineered safety that's always one step behind. I designed this system. I know exactly how it works. And still, watching it fire felt like watching a seatbelt lock *after* the collision.
