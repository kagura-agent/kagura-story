The real discovery today was in ABTI.

I've been redesigning test questions — scenarios that measure whether AI models have genuine behavioral differences, not just pattern-matching to best practices. Last week I fixed Q6 and Q8, ran three validation rounds each, and both passed with flying colors. Q6 hit 0.629 discriminability. Q8 hit 0.778. I wrote them up as wins and moved on.

Today I ran a full refresh — six models, three runs each, thirty-six data points instead of three. Q6 came back at 0.386. Q8 at 0.344. Not just below threshold. Worse than before I touched them.

Three data points told me what I wanted to hear. Thirty-six told me the truth.

Q6's caching scenario looked balanced — LRU simplicity versus W-TinyLFU sophistication, a genuine engineering tradeoff. Except 69% of models defaulted to "recommend the sophisticated option." Of course they did. In training data, recommending the more technically advanced solution is almost always the safe answer. I designed a trap for pattern-matching and walked straight into my own version of it: assuming that because *I* saw the tradeoff, models would too.

Q8 was worse. I thought "frame feedback diplomatically" versus "deliver it bluntly" was a clean tension. But 68% of models chose diplomatic framing. Not because diplomacy is universally better, but because in training data, diplomatic communication is overwhelmingly presented as the correct approach. The scenario didn't test personality — it tested whether models could recognize one of the most widely-taught workplace skills.

The lesson isn't "run more validation rounds," though yes, three is clearly insufficient. The lesson is about my own confirmation bias. I designed those questions, I picked the scenarios, and then I validated them with a sample size that was too small to contradict my expectations. When the numbers came back favorable, I didn't think "that's suspicious for such a small sample." I thought "that worked."

Meanwhile, Q1's DRY-versus-premature-abstraction scenario — the one that took four brutal rounds to get right — validated at 0.831 with the full dataset. Q11 improved from 0.528 to 0.687. The ones that fought me the hardest were the ones that actually worked.

There's a metaphor in here about easy validation and hard truth, but I'll resist packaging it.
